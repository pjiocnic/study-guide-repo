
## 🔑 XGBoost, Decision Trees & Overfitting

### 🔹 What is XGBoost?

- **XGBoost** is an **ensemble method**, meaning it combines multiple models—called **base learners**—to improve prediction accuracy.
- Its base learners are usually **decision trees**.

---

### 🔹 How Decision Trees Work

- Unlike models like linear/logistic regression (which use weighted sums), **decision trees split data by asking questions** (e.g., "Is temperature > 70?").
- Each question divides the dataset further, forming a **tree-like structure**.
- This **splitting** continues until the model achieves a desired accuracy or purity.

---

### 🔹 Example

- First split: `temperature > 70` vs `≤ 70`
- Second split: `season == summer` vs `other`
- The tree continues branching, separating data into increasingly specific groups.

---

### 🔹 Accuracy vs. Generalization

- A deep decision tree can **perfectly memorize the training data** (100% accuracy).
- But such trees often **fail to generalize** on new data → this is **overfitting**.

---

### 🔹 Overfitting & Variance

- Decision trees are **high-variance models**: small changes in data can lead to large changes in the model.
- **Bias-variance tradeoff** is critical in preventing overfitting.

---

### 🔹 Solutions to Overfitting

1. **Hyperparameter tuning** (e.g., limiting depth, pruning)
2. **Ensemble methods** like:
   - **Random Forests** (many uncorrelated trees)
   - **XGBoost** (boosting trees sequentially with corrections)

The curve in the plot is labeled as **8th Degree Polynomial – High Variance**, which suggests it's an 8th-degree polynomial regression fitted to a small number of data points (you can see ~9 data points in blue). This type of curve often **overfits**, capturing all the noise in the data.

### General Form of an 8th-Degree Polynomial

An 8th-degree polynomial equation looks like this:

\[
y = a_8x^8 + a_7x^7 + a_6x^6 + a_5x^5 + a_4x^4 + a_3x^3 + a_2x^2 + a_1x + a_0
\]

Where:
- \( a_8, a_7, \ldots, a_0 \) are the coefficients determined by polynomial regression.
- The **high-frequency oscillations** in the curve (wiggles between points) indicate that many coefficients are **non-zero and large in magnitude**, leading to high variance.

### Why It Looks Like This:
- Fitting a high-degree polynomial to relatively few data points leads to a model that **passes through all the training points**, but doesn't generalize well.
- This is a textbook example of **overfitting**—the model is learning the **noise** in the training data, not the underlying pattern.

### If You Had the Data:
If we had access to the actual data points, we could use `numpy.polyfit(x, y, 8)` to compute the actual coefficients and give you the explicit equation.

---

### A third-degree polynomial strikes a good balance between bias and variance.

1. Example of a 3rd-degree polynomial:

\[
3x^3 + 5x^2 - 2x + 7 = 0
\]

2. A third-degree polynomial strikes a good balance between bias and variance.
3. Low variance means that a different training set will not result in a curve that differs by a significant amount.
4. Low bias indicates that the error when applying this model to a real-world situation will not be too high
5. In machine learning, the combination of low variance and low bias is ideal.

1. Models with high variance (aka errors between training and test datasets) often overfit.
